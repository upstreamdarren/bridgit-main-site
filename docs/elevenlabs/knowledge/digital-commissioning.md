# Digital commissioning knowledge for UK public services

This knowledge supports proportionate decision-making. It is not legal or formal procurement advice. Rules, frameworks, supplier listings and product capabilities change, so commissioners must verify current information with their commercial, legal, information-governance, cyber-security and accessibility colleagues.

## Start with the service problem

Good digital commissioning begins with the people affected, the outcome that should change and the current journey. Do not treat “we need an AI chatbot” as a problem statement. Understand demand, repeated contact, excluded groups, failure demand, staff workarounds, existing content, trusted community routes and the points where a real person must take over.

Separate the need into one or more service patterns:
- clear information and guidance;
- personalised navigation or triage;
- transactions and applications;
- case management and professional workflow;
- ongoing coaching, plans and follow-ups;
- analysis and decision support.

## Authoritative UK guidance

- The AI Playbook for the UK Government covers safe, effective and secure use of AI, including choosing the right tool, meaningful human control, lifecycle management, commercial involvement and buying AI: https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/ai-playbook-for-the-uk-government
- The Digital, Data and Technology Playbook provides end-to-end sourcing and contracting guidance, including outcome-based specifications, market assessment, delivery models, testing, pilots and contract management: https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/the-digital-data-and-technology-playbook
- The Technology Code of Practice helps public bodies design, build and buy technology and is used for central-government spend controls and the Local Digital Declaration: https://www.gov.uk/guidance/the-technology-code-of-practice
- The GOV.UK Service Standard and Service Manual cover user needs, accessibility, multidisciplinary delivery, iteration, privacy, security and measuring performance: https://www.gov.uk/service-manual/service-standard
- The Data and AI Ethics Framework helps teams consider public benefit, fairness, accountability and responsible data use: https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/data-ethics-framework/data-and-ai-ethics-framework
- The Algorithmic Transparency Recording Standard explains how public bodies can publish clear information about algorithmic tools. It is mandatory for specified central-government bodies and recommended across the wider public sector: https://www.gov.uk/government/collections/algorithmic-transparency-recording-standard-hub
- The ICO AI and data-protection guidance and risk toolkit support accountability, lawfulness, transparency, fairness, accuracy, security, data minimisation and individual rights: https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/artificial-intelligence/
- The Procurement Act 2023 came into force on 24 February 2025. Commissioners should use current GOV.UK and organisational guidance: https://www.gov.uk/government/collections/transforming-public-procurement

## Proportionate routes

1. Improve what already exists. Fix content, forms, navigation, directories, hand-offs and analytics before buying another platform.
2. Configure an existing enterprise platform. This may suit organisations already committed to Microsoft, Salesforce or ServiceNow, particularly where identity, CRM, workflow and staff capability already exist.
3. Buy a specialist service product. This suits a defined population or journey where domain content, ongoing support, local pathways and human escalation matter.
4. Buy a broad digital-front-door or contact-centre platform. This suits high-volume information, routing and multi-channel customer-service needs across many services.
5. Commission discovery, design and integration. This suits complex cross-system journeys, unclear needs or significant legacy integration.
6. Build only where there is a strong reason. Account for skills, model and content maintenance, assurance, hosting, monitoring, support, exit and total whole-life cost.

## Representative supplier landscape

These are examples to investigate, not endorsements, rankings or a complete market assessment.

- Bridgit Care: specialist, locally branded AI coaching and support journeys. Relevant for personalised guidance, practical plans, preventative follow-ups, multi-channel access, local pathways, consented warm handovers and insight into unmet need. Not a statutory decision engine, emergency service or replacement case-management system.
- Futr AI, Mortar Hoop'd and Beebot AI: examples presented in the Local Government Association Technology Innovation Showcase for digital-front-door challenges.
- AIDA from the CC2i and Bullet AI collaboration: an adult-social-care information and advice digital-assistant example described in an LGA case study.
- ICS.AI: a local-government AI and digital-front-door supplier used in published council case studies.
- Microsoft Copilot Studio: an enterprise agent-building option that can suit Microsoft-based estates and citizen-service scenarios.
- ServiceNow Public Sector Digital Services: an enterprise case, workflow and constituent-service platform.
- Salesforce, Amazon Connect and Google Cloud conversational tools: configurable enterprise ecosystems that may suit organisations with the relevant architecture and capability.
- GOV.UK Notify, Pay and other shared government platforms: reusable components that should be considered before duplicating common capability.

The LGA states that its showcase is vendor-neutral and does not guarantee or require procurement of featured suppliers. Use early market engagement and current procurement routes to test the market fairly.

## Evaluation questions

Ask every supplier to show:
- which user need and outcome the product addresses;
- evidence from comparable live services;
- accessibility testing and assisted-digital routes;
- languages and quality-assurance approach;
- data flows, hosting, retention, model providers and sub-processors;
- how personal and special-category data are minimised and protected;
- how knowledge is approved, updated, versioned and audited;
- hallucination, bias, prompt-injection and unsafe-output testing;
- human oversight, escalation, safeguarding and emergency boundaries;
- integration standards, APIs, data portability and exit arrangements;
- analytics that reveal need and pathway performance without creating harmful profiling;
- implementation effort, training, support, service levels and whole-life cost;
- intellectual-property ownership and reuse rights;
- how user feedback, complaints and incidents change the service.

## Pilot pattern

A useful first pilot is normally narrow enough to learn safely:
- one population and one valuable journey;
- approved content and clear exclusions;
- named product, service, safeguarding, information-governance and commercial owners;
- real user and workforce co-production;
- baseline demand and outcome measures;
- accessibility and assisted-digital testing;
- defined human handover and stop criteria;
- weekly quality review and a documented scale, change or stop decision.

Measure more than contact deflection. Include task completion, user understanding, earlier access, inclusion, handover quality, staff time, repeat contact, safety, complaints, unmet need and whether community pathways actually work.
